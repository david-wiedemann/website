Overview

Homogenization studies equations in materials with very fine spatial structures and derives effective macroscopic models that retain the essential influence of those structures. A material with a very small, but positive, characteristic length scale \(\varepsilon\) can be approximated by a homogeneous substitute material: by artificially letting \(\varepsilon\to0\), one derives effective equations for the original medium without resolving each microscopic detail. My research focuses in particular on media with time-evolving geometries and on metamaterials, including effective transport, fluid flow, and electromagnetic properties.

A fundamental example is periodic homogenization of a stationary elliptic conductivity problem. The material consists of two components with different conductivities, periodically arranged on the small length scale \(\varepsilon\). Although the physical material has a fixed, small \(\varepsilon>0\), we consider the idealized limit \(\varepsilon\to0\) to obtain a homogeneous substitute material. Its effective conductivity is described by the constant tensor \(A^{\mathrm{hom}}\), which incorporates the influence of the unresolved microscopic phases.

Microscopic equation

\[ -\nabla\cdot\!\left[a\!\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\nabla u_\varepsilon\right]=f \quad\text{in }\Omega, \qquad u_\varepsilon=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega. \]
\(a(y)\) is \(Y\)-periodic, \(0<\alpha\leq a(y)\leq\beta\), and \(Y=(0,1)^2\).

Effective equation

\[ -\nabla\cdot(A^{\mathrm{hom}}\nabla u_0)=f\quad\text{in }\Omega, \qquad u_0=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega. \]

Effective coefficient

\[ A_{ij}^{\mathrm{hom}}=\frac{1}{|Y|}\int_Y a(y) \left(\delta_{ij}+\frac{\partial\chi_j}{\partial y_i}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y, \qquad i,j=1,2. \]

Cell problem

\[ -\nabla_y\cdot\bigl[a(y)(e_j+\nabla_y\chi_j)\bigr]=0 \quad\text{in }Y, \]
\(\chi_j\) is \(Y\)-periodic, \(\int_Y\chi_j\,\mathrm{d}y=0\), \(j=1,2\).

The cell correctors \(\chi_j\) capture how the periodic microstructure distorts a unit macroscopic gradient in direction \(e_j\). Averaging the resulting microscopic flux over one unit cell yields \(A^{\mathrm{hom}}\). In general, this tensor is anisotropic, even when the local conductivity \(a(y)\) is a scalar. This stationary example illustrates the basic mechanism underlying more elaborate homogenization problems, including evolving microstructures, fluid flow, and effective interface conditions.
